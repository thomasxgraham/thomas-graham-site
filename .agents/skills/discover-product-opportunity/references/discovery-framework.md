# Discovery Framework

## Customer journey

Map:

- trigger and desired outcome;
- actors and affected people;
- steps, decisions, channels, handoffs, delays, and emotions;
- current tools, workarounds, and the choice to do nothing;
- moments of pain, risk, trust, or abandonment;
- observable behaviors and lifecycle states;
- evidence source and freshness for every material claim.

Use the level of detail appropriate to product familiarity. Existing-product work may begin with known behavior; a new customer or market requires earlier research.

## Problem selection

Assess each candidate problem:

1. How painful is it?
2. How much would a strong solution improve the customer's life or work?
3. How frequently does it occur?
4. How is it solved today, including workarounds and inaction?
5. What capabilities and advantages does the team have?
6. Who else is positioned to solve it?
7. What could cause the effort to fail?
8. What behavior change or trust is required?
9. Is there a step-change opportunity and meaningful differentiation?

## Interview craft

- Research the participant and context before high-stakes interviews.
- Explain what is being learned and how notes or recordings will be used.
- Obtain appropriate consent.
- Begin with broad, neutral prompts.
- Mix open and closed questions deliberately.
- Do not mention the hoped-for answer inside the question.
- Let the participant finish; do not fill every silence.
- Follow important threads and ask why until the root cause is clear.
- Update the guide as repeated interviews reveal better questions.
- Preserve detailed notes, contradictions, and negative cases.

Five interviews can be a useful directional starting point from the course exercise, but sample design must follow decision risk, population diversity, saturation, and research constraints rather than a universal quota.

## Personas

Build personas from patterns that affect product value or use:

- characteristics and behaviors;
- motivations and goals;
- context, constraints, technology, and risk posture;
- current alternatives;
- user, buyer, approver, administrator, or affected-party role;
- how the persona can be recognized in real data or operations;
- evidence strength, exceptions, and overlap with other personas.

Reject decorative personas that cannot be identified or do not change a product decision.
