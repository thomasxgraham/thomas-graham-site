# Prioritization Framework

## Factor design

Derive factors from the strategy rather than importing a generic score. Consider:

- each objective or KR separately;
- customer pain, reach, impact, and evidence;
- mission or strategic alignment;
- engineering effort and feasibility;
- direct financial and operating cost;
- delay or time-based risk;
- differentiation and current alternatives;
- integration, scalability, adoption, and support;
- industry, customer, regulatory, country, language, accessibility, and equity;
- AI data rights, evaluation readiness, safety, human oversight, model/vendor risk, latency, and cost.

Document factor definition, direction, scale, weight, evidence source, and owner. Avoid double-counting correlated factors.

## Critical decisions

Before drawing a roadmap, identify one to three decisions. For each capture:

- why the decision is critical;
- what information would improve it;
- who benefits, loses, or bears risk;
- decision owner and deadline;
- reversible versus irreversible aspects.

## Stakeholder allocation

Use a fixed point budget, such as 100 points, to force tradeoffs. Preserve individual and total allocations. Treat the result as structured input, not a vote that automatically decides the roadmap.

## Precision

Use coarse sizing when uncertainty is high. A transparent directional estimate is better than false precision. Record confidence and sensitivity; close rankings require judgment and more evidence.

## Visual roadmap

Choose the smallest useful horizon. Organize by outcome, investment category, product surface, or dependency. Show approximate allocation of effort and include a concise rationale describing factors, tradeoffs, and why the plan is appropriate.

## Cornell grounding

This framework synthesizes the CTECH103 factor, decision, stakeholder-allocation, course-project, and workbook artifacts.
